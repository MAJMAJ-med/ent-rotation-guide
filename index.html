PART I. HEAD & NECK ANATOMY TO KNOW COLD

▸

Carotid bifurcation and the neck

  • CCA bifurcates at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage, about C3–C4 (variable, C3–C5; do not quote one level as gospel). ICA posterolateral, ECA anteromedial.
  • Carotid bulb = proximal ICA = the plaque site; seat of the carotid sinus (baroreceptor, CN IX) and carotid body (chemoreceptor, CN IX; the carotid-body paraganglioma arises here).
  • Two guaranteed pimps: the first ECA branch is the superior thyroid artery; the ICA has zero branches in the neck (its first branch, the ophthalmic, is intracranial). On angiography the branchy neck vessel is the ECA and the smooth one to the skull base is the ICA.
  • ECA branches, “Some Anatomists Like Freaking Out Poor Medical Students”: Superior thyroid, Ascending pharyngeal, Lingual, Facial, Occipital, Posterior auricular, Maxillary, Superficial temporal. The maxillary gives the middle meningeal (epidural hematoma) and the sphenopalatine, the “artery of epistaxis.”
  • ICA vs ECA on duplex: the ICA shows a low-resistance waveform (high diastolic flow, it feeds the brain); the ECA shows a high-resistance waveform, and a temporal tap confirms the ECA.
  • Left vs right RLN asymmetry: the left recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the aortic arch (a long mediastinal course), so a left mediastinal or aortic process can cause hoarseness (cardiovocal / Ortner syndrome); the right RLN loops under the right subclavian artery.

Cranial nerves of the neck (your ENT cranial-nerve exam)

Nerve Injury sign Where it is at risk
Hypoglossal (XII) Tongue deviates toward the lesion Crosses the ICA/ECA high, about 2 cm above the bifurcation
Vagus (X) / RLN fibers Hoarseness In the carotid sheath (posterior, between artery and IJV); vagal injury, not direct RLN injury, is the commonest cause of post-CEA hoarseness
Superior laryngeal n. (external br.) Voice fatigue, loss of high pitch (“opera singer’s nerve”) Runs with the superior thyroid artery at the superior pole
Marginal mandibular br. (VII) Asymmetric lower lip and smile High incision or retraction near the mandibular angle
Glossopharyngeal (IX) Dysphagia Very high ICA dissection
Spinal accessory (XI) Trapezius weakness, shoulder drop Posterior triangle, the neck-dissection nerve
Ansa cervicalis Minimal (strap motor) On the anterior sheath; can be divided for high exposure

Carotid sheath = “VAN”: Vein (IJV, lateral), Artery (carotid, medial), Nerve (vagus, posterior between them). The sympathetic chain runs behind the sheath, so injury there causes Horner syndrome.

Aortic arch, arteria lusoria, and the non-recurrent nerve

  • Arch branch order, “ABC”: brachiocephalic (innominate), then left common carotid, then left subclavian. A bovine arch (about 13–15%) is the commonest variant.
  • An aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria) causes dysphagia lusoria and is paired with a non-recurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve, a classic thyroid-surgery trap: on the right the nerve branches straight off the vagus instead of looping under the subclavian.

Instruments and suture (the free-flap transfer)

Instrument Role
DeBakey forceps atraumatic pickups (never grab a vessel with toothed forceps)
Castroviejo needle driver / scissors micro instruments (the same ones used under the scope for microvascular work)
Bulldog clamp soft, atraumatic vessel occlusion
Potts scissors extend the arteriotomy or venotomy
Vessel loops single = retract, double = occlude
Weitlaner / Gelpi self-retaining retractors
  • Suture for a microvascular anastomosis: permanent monofilament (nylon or Prolene) on a tapered needle, with sizes stepping down to 8-0, 9-0, 10-0 for free-flap vessels. Double-armed, parachute the posterior wall, forehand one wall and backhand the other, lay throws flat with constant tension, back-bleed before the final tie, then confirm flow.

The two genuine vascular-to-ENT transfer clusters

  1. The carotid and neck block. A carotid exposure is, anatomically, a neck dissection: the bifurcation, the ICA/ECA, the cranial nerves above, the sheath (VAN), and the IJV (the free-flap recipient vein). Microvascular free flaps share this turf: radial-forearm, fibula, and ALT flaps anastomose to ECA branches (facial, superior thyroid) with jugular venous outflow.
  2. Fine-tissue and monofilament skill. Atraumatic handling under loupes or the scope, no-touch intima, slick monofilament technique, and suction as a skill (clear the field without obscuring the surgeon’s view). These are exactly the skills the acting internship rewards.
Emergency

Airway emergency. An expanding neck hematoma after neck or thyroid surgery is an airway emergency: open the incision at the bedside to decompress the hematoma before moving to definitive control in the OR.

Head & neck anatomy

Q A
First branch of the ECA? Superior thyroid artery (SALFOPMS)
ICA branches in the neck? None (the first is the ophthalmic, intracranial)
Carotid sheath contents? VAN: carotid (medial), IJV (lateral), vagus (posterior between them)
Tongue deviation with a hypoglossal (XII) injury? Toward the side of the lesion
Commonest cause of hoarseness after a carotid endarterectomy? Vagal stretch, not direct RLN injury
Why is the left RLN more vulnerable to a chest process? It loops under the aortic arch (long mediastinal course), so a left mediastinal or aortic lesion causes hoarseness (cardiovocal / Ortner)
Artery of epistaxis? Sphenopalatine (terminal branch of the maxillary artery)
An aberrant right subclavian artery is paired with which nerve variant? A non-recurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve (arteria lusoria)
